First principles

GST is a destination tax, so a state's revenue should track the size of its economy. Every rupee of SGST can be split into two factors, and any gap with another state has to sit in one of them:

SGST = GSDP × (taxpayers ÷ GSDP) × (SGST ÷ taxpayer)

The economy (GSDP) is given. The registration base and the tax collected per registrant are what the department can move. The sections below test each one.

Method, sources and caveats
  • GST figures are from GSTN's state-wise collection, IGST settlement and return-filing files, FY 2018-19 to 2025-26. Collections exclude cess, which is not reported for 2025-26.
  • Own SGST is SGST paid by taxpayers registered in the state. State revenue adds the state's share of IGST settlement. For 2025-26 and April–October 2026 it uses the official post-settlement table. Earlier years are SGST + settlement from the GSTN files, which match the official table for 2023-24 and 2024-25.
  • GSDP at current prices is from MoSPI as compiled by StatisticsTimes: all states for 2024-25 and 2025-26, and yearly series for West Bengal and Gujarat. These are estimates and get revised.
  • Taxpayers are the largest number eligible to file GSTR-3B in the last three return periods of each year.
  • Intensity ratios assume each state's taxable consumption moves with its GSDP. A state that exports more of its output, or has a larger exempt or informal economy, will show lower intensity for reasons the department cannot change. Part of the gap is structural, which is why the simulator lets you choose how much of it to close.
  • Summary charts: growth is compared with Odisha, a comparable eastern state. The "best state" on share of GSDP is the highest among large states (2025-26 GSDP of ₹2 lakh Cr or more); on total collection this reflects corporate headquarters, so it marks a ceiling, not a target.
  • The six-lever amounts come from DCT Tableau reports and are leads to verify. They overlap and should not be added together.
